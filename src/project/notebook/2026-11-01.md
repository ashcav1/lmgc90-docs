## How do we know the last contact is the last?

The question behind every "bounced off". After its last touch the craft is
in free flight, and a free body feels only gravity, which exerts no torque:
its centre of mass follows a ballistic arc and its rotation obeys Euler's
equations for a torque-free body (the spin axis wobbles because the three
moments of inertia differ). LMGC90 integrates the same equations, with
the gyroscopic torque as an explicit term (`comp_Fext_RBDY3`). So the
craft's future after a drop is known exactly, and can be checked against
the whole bed rather than predicted.

`src/milani/flight.py` integrates it (RK4, steps of at most 0.02 s) and
was first compared with the simulation's own recorded free flights: over
12 drops of bed 1 with 7-70 s of free flight each, the predicted centre
stays within 0.001 mm and the orientation within 0.06 degrees of
LMGC90's. On one drop, every point of the craft's surface is within
0.015 mm after 48 s.

The flight is moved over the bed in two stages. The bed is kept as three
layers: the patch's free grains, the fixed ring, and the grains outside
the simulation.

1. **Coarse:** points every 2 cm on the craft's surface are compared with
   each layer's top surface on the 1 cm grid, each cell raised to its
   highest neighbour.
2. **Exact:** wherever a point comes within 2 cm, the exact distance
   between the craft's convex pieces and the nearby grains' convex
   polyhedra decides. That distance is the closest pair of points of two
   convex hulls, a small least-squares problem; on 1000 pairs of boxes with
   known distances its worst error is 2e-13 m. Near a grain, every step is
   the longest in which the craft cannot close the distance (conservative
   advancement), so no touch falls between two tests.

A touch is an exact distance under 1 mm.

Two things nearly went wrong on the way:

- scipy's `nnls` fails to converge on 10-15 % of touching boxes. Bounded
  least squares (`lsq_linear`, BVLS) fails on none and takes 0.15 ms.
- The `divide by zero / overflow encountered in matmul` warnings that
  appeared with the first version are a numpy 2.0 + Apple Accelerate
  artefact. The products are exact (4e-16 from a reference).

## The craft turned about the wrong point

The first exact check said drop b955841420ca (bed 1) would touch a free
grain 4 s after its run had ended. Re-simulated past that moment, it never
did, and the solver's own gap to that boulder never went below 2.3 mm
(9.4 mm at the predicted moment). So the two geometries disagreed. Placing
the craft from craft.json next to the craft in the run's ParaView frames
showed a constant offset of 10.172 mm along the craft's body x axis, at every frame.

The cause is in LMGC90's reader (mod_RBDY3, `read_bodies`). A body made of
several contactors is centred on the volume-weighted centroid of their
geometric volumes, while the principal inertia it is given is kept. The
pre side sets each piece's "effective volume" from its mass, and LMGC90
never sees it: it recomputes every polyhedron's volume from its vertices.
The craft with panels open is a 10.8 kg bus and two 1 cm, 0.6 kg solar panels, far lighter
for their volume. In every open-panel drop so far it therefore turned about
a point 10.2 mm from its centre of mass, toward the solar panels. The folded craft
(one piece) was right.

The fix (`impactors.ballast_piece`) puts a box inside the craft's body, 3.9 x 24 x
36 cm and 3 mm inside every wall, so that no grain can reach it before the
bus. Its volume moves the geometric centroid exactly onto the centre of
mass, and the density becomes 12 kg / total geometric volume. In pre the
ballast must be massless. The first attempt gave it zero inertia, and pre
treats any inertia below 1e-6 as "not given" and computes both volume and
inertia from the geometry, so the ballast came out heavy: the centre moved
7.17 mm and the inertia grew 7 %. It now gets 2e-6, and the craft's body 2e-6 less;
`build_avatar` asserts that pre's centre and inertia are the intended ones.

Checked in a run: the simulated craft sits within 1.6e-11 m of its design,
with inertia 0.4535 / 0.1531 / 0.3387 kg m^2 (intended 0.4534 / 0.1531 /
0.3386). Every drop now records this check (`setup.craft_geometry_error_m`)
and warns above 0.1 mm. On one 4 m-bed drop the fix moved e from 0.300 to
0.329 and the last touch from 3.50 to 3.78 s.

Re-checked with the craft where LMGC90 really had it, the 93 bounced-off
drops made before tonight:

|---|---|
| touch nothing on the bed again | 75 |

## A drop now ends on its exact flight

When the craft has been free for 0.5 s and is rising, its flight is
scanned. The drop goes on while the flight comes within 1 cm, plus the
fastest free grain's surface speed times the time ahead, of a grain the
simulation can move: the solver decides those, also when the craft's
centre has already crossed the patch's edge but a solar panel is still over it.
Otherwise the drop is over:

- `clear`: no touch on the bed in the time the run could still take;
- `beyond`: the next touch is on the fixed ring or the bed outside the
  patch. The prediction is stored with the drop (`contact.flight`).

The grain-speed allowance came from the first re-simulation. One drop was
declared over 1 s after its last touch while passing 10.08 mm from a free
grain, and one second after an impact the grains are still moving.

This replaces both the circumsphere test of the evening and the
"airborne over the patch edge" rule. Results are identical where the
physics is unchanged (craft with panels folded: e to five digits), and drops end 0.5-1 s
after their last touch instead of tens of seconds later: a 4 m-bed drop
took 21 s of computing instead of 134 s.

`lab.py check-last-contact` now runs the same exact flight from the last
touch over the whole bed, seconds per drop:

- **Until the run's end:** only the grains outside the simulation are
  checked, because inside it the solver decided.
- **After the end:** all three layers are checked, the free grains at their
  final pose. Impact runs now write a ParaView frame at the very end for
  this.
- **The replay** (detachment off, 150 s more) runs where the flight passes
  within 5 cm of a free grain, because only there can the grains' own later
  motion matter; `--replay all` replays every drop.

Verdicts: confirmed, next touch off the patch, lands back on the patch,
touched again.

## Settling at higher friction does not help (1 November)

The 3 m bed with a thin top layer (`bed4u_3m`) settles slowly: 11-12 s per step, about
5,000 solver iterations per step (the 2 m bed with small stones needed about 2,800),
5-25 % of steps stopping unconverged at the 10,000-iteration limit, the
fastest grain spinning at 50-260 rad/s, and the median move still 21 mm/s
after 12 s. Could a higher friction while settling (then the impact
friction, 0.6, for the drops) end the chatter sooner?

Test: the same recipe on a 2 m × 2 m bed (206 boulders + 1,132 small stones),
`configs/experiments/bed4u_2m_mu06|08|09.toml`, identical except
`settle_friction`.

| friction | seed | boulder stage | small-stone stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.6 (control) | 1 | rest after 8,400 steps | running: 20.5 mm/s at 14 s, 4,300 iterations per step (full bed: 21 mm/s at 12 s, 5,000) |
| 0.8 | 1 | rest after 12,500 steps | solver diverged at step ~300, as the small stones landed (twice) |
| 0.9 | 1 | solver diverged at step ~950 (twice) | - |
| 0.9 | 2 | solver diverged at step ~5,200, nearly at rest (twice) | - |

Every run at 0.8 or above ended in an NLGS divergence ("NLGS diverged at
ITERATION 510": a NaN in the contact reactions); at 0.6 none did. Where a
higher-friction stage did finish, it took 50 % more steps, not fewer: the
median move of the 0.8 boulders stayed 1.5-3 times the control's at equal
times. This is not the big-POLYR detection failure of 30 October (that one
diverged at iteration 10; the big-POLYR count is 0 in these decks).

Settle friction stays equal to the impact friction (0.6). A friction
switched after settling would also have left the bed out of balance at
the drops' friction. The 2 m control reproduces the full bed's slow
settle, so it is kept as the quick test bed for this problem.

## Plain words, and sizes that are lengths (1 November)

The pages used the code's names for things (fines, veneer, substrate, crop,
frozen ring, stowed, deployed, gap and velocity law, ladder, battery,
skeleton) and gave each grain's size as its long semi-axis, half its
length. A "0.15-0.35 m boulder" is 30-70 cm long. Measured on the settled
beds, real grains come out about 10 % shorter than twice that (19-69 cm on
bed 1, median 42 x 30 x 20 cm), because only 10-12 corners outline each
one. Two listings were simply wrong: the 3 m bed's boulders were given as
"0.065-0.40 m" (its file says 0.10-0.35 m, 20-70 cm long), and one note
called the 2 m bed's boulders "10-25 cm across" (they are 20-50 cm long).

Every page now uses one vocabulary, defined in the
[glossary](../glossary.md): small stones (8-16 cm long; they are not
fine-grained, and nothing finer is simulated), thin top layer, thick top
layer, bottom layer, even size mix, realistic size mix, patch, fixed ring,
panels open, panels folded, position rule, speed rule, speed limit,
500-step check, study beds 1-6, 36-drop set. The code keeps its names; the
glossary says which code name each word stands for. Bed and drop IDs are
unchanged (checked on all 52 settled beds).

One consequence worth stating plainly: the thin top layer (8 cm of stones
5-10 cm thick) is about one stone deep, so the boulder tops stick out, and
the craft's first touch on the 2 m bed was a boulder in all 12 drops.

## How big must a bed be? (1 November)

A drop simulates only the patch, a circle of free grains around the
landing spot, with a fixed ring around it. The bed must hold:

- the patch: radius 1.2 m with the panels open (the craft is 1.47 m
  across, reach 0.76 m), 0.7 m folded (reach 0.23 m);
- 0.75 m between the patch and each wall: one largest boulder (70 cm) plus
  5 cm, because grains next to a wall packed against it;
- the spread of the landing spots: 0.6 m for the 36-drop set's 3 x 3 grid.

Side = 2 x (patch radius + 0.75 m) + spread: 4.5 m with the panels open,
3.0 m folded with spots 5 cm apart, and a 2 m box leaves a 0.25 m patch,
too small even for the folded craft. The box costs settle time once; a
drop's cost is set by the patch (median wall time on bed 1: 15 s for a
0.45 m patch with the panels folded, 220 s for a 1.2 m patch with them
open). The study beds therefore stay 4.5 m x 4.5 m, the smallest box for
the open-panel craft and the 3 x 3 grid, and 2 m beds are not used for
drops.

What grain motion says about the patch: in all four laptop sets, the share
of grains that moved more than 1 mm falls from 33-75 % near the landing
spot to 9-37 % in the outer fifth of the patch, and more than 5 mm from
6-45 % to 1-10 %. Before the craft arrives the grains drift at 0.001-0.01
mm/s. So the impact's disturbance fades toward the edge but has not died
out there. Whether that changes the bounce is the patch-size test.

## Is the patch big enough? (1 November, gate D12)

The same 20 drops on the laptop copy of study bed 1 (5 landing spots 4 cm
apart x 4 and 8 cm/s x 0 and 40 degrees), changing only the patch radius
(`configs/experiments/patch_*.toml`). Every first touch was identical at
every patch size.

| craft | patch radius | grains free | median e, 0 / 40 degrees | left the patch | computing per drop |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| panels open | 0.9 m | 95 | 0.213 / 0.315 | 6 of 20 | 100 s |
| panels open | 1.2 m (in use) | 167 | 0.220 / 0.318 | 3 of 20 | 240 s |
| panels open | 1.45 m | 246 | 0.229 / 0.317 | 3 of 20 | 307 s |
| panels folded | 0.45 m | 26 | 0.250 / 0.402 | 2 of 20 | 15 s |
| panels folded | 0.7 m (in use on 3 m beds) | 58 | 0.210 / 0.375 | 0 of 20 | 37 s |
| panels folded | 1.0 m | 115 | 0.273 / 0.448 | 0 of 20 | 58 s |

(e: speed ratio, medians over the drops that bounced off.)

- **Panels open: 1.2 m is enough.** Drop by drop, against the 1.45 m patch,
  the speed ratio changes by a median of 0.000 (95 % interval -0.005 to
  +0.010) at 1.2 m and +0.007 (-0.009 to +0.025) at 0.9 m: smaller than the
  spread between landing spots 4 cm apart (0.03). What a smaller patch does
  change is how many drops are cut off: at 0.9 m twice as many slid out of
  the patch (6 of 20, against 3 at both 1.2 and 1.45 m). A bigger patch
  buys nothing, so the study beds need no bigger box: 4.5 m x 4.5 m stays.
- **Panels folded: single drops are far more sensitive,** with the same
  first touch every time. The speed ratio of a given drop moves by a median
  of 0.05 and up to 0.27 between patch sizes, and the spread between spots
  4 cm apart is 0.08 (0.03 with the panels open). The folded craft hits
  with one edge or corner and the result hangs on that one contact; the
  open craft lands on a panel over several touches, which averages it out.
  The 0.7 m patch gave speed ratios 0.04 lower than the 1.0 m patch (95 %
  interval 0.004 to 0.08), while the 0.45 m patch did not differ from
  1.0 m. A real patch effect would shrink as the patch grows, so this is
  more likely the folded craft's sensitivity in 20 drops on one small area
  than a bias, but it is not settled. Treat folded-craft medians from the
  3 m beds as uncertain by about ±0.05. The 0.45 m patch also lost 2 of 20
  drops out of the patch.
- **Consequences.** Study beds: 4.5 m x 4.5 m with a 1.2 m patch, as they
  are. Laptop tests with the panels folded: 3 m x 3 m with a 0.7 m patch,
  for comparing beds, not for paper numbers. No 2 m beds for drops. Any
  comparison of single drops (before / after a code change) needs many
  drops, not one, above all with the panels folded.

## How buried are the boulders? (1 November, night)

Measured on the height map of each settled bed (the top of the bed seen
from above, 1 cm grid, the outer 15 % of each side left out): which grain
forms the top at each point, and for each point where a boulder shows, how
far it stands above the mean height of the small stones within a 30 cm square around it (`beds.surface_burial`, now on every bed report).

| bed | small stones poured | small stones cover | boulders that show stand above the stones |
|---|---|---|---|
| quick 2 m bed (`bed4q_2m`): 0.5 m of boulders 20-50 cm long, split contact rule | 8 cm | **24 %** | median 9 cm, 1 in 4 more than 16 cm, 1 in 20 more than 23 cm |
| old 2 m test (`exp_fines`): same boulders, speed rule on every pair | 10 cm | 77 % | median -2 cm: the boulders sank into the stones (9 cm overlaps, quality checks FAIL) |

So the thin top layer, settled the way we settle beds now, leaves three
quarters of the surface bare boulder. The only bed that looked buried got
there by an overlap artefact. The surface the boulders make is rough: its
height varies by 11-14 cm (standard deviation) on every boulder bed
measured (study bed 1 at 4.5 m, bed 2's boulders at 3 m, the 2 m beds).
The stones roll off boulder tops into the gaps between them, so to bury the
boulders the stones must fill those gaps up to near the boulder tops.

**What a buried bed costs.** A 25 cm layer of stones 8-16 cm long on a 4.5
m bed is about 17,900 stones (study bed 6, `lab.py describe`); stones 10-20
cm long halve that. Finer stones are out of reach: halving the size
multiplies the count by eight.

## Beds with small stones do not come to rest (1 November, night)

The 3 m bed (`bed4u_3m`: study bed 1's boulders, 0.9 m, and an 8 cm thin top
layer; 463 boulders and 2,547 small stones) was stopped at 22:00 after 32
hours, at step 9,700 of its small-stone stage. Its median grain still
moved 11 mm/s (the rest test is 3 mm/s), falling 2 % per 300 steps. Its
2 m version (`bed4u_2m_mu06`) was at 18 mm/s after 7,200 steps. The quick 2
m bed with smaller boulders (20-50 cm, 0.5 m deep) rested at step 6,900.

What the stalled beds share, from their progress records:

- at least 1 grain in 100 at the 0.3 m/s speed limit at every check, the
  whole time; mean grain speed 7-14 cm/s; the fastest grain spinning
  at 30-160 rad/s;
- about 2,000 contact pairs overlapping by more than 0.1 mm, the deepest
  6-40 mm;
- 600-5,900 solver passes per time step (the budget allows 10,000), so a
  step costs 11.6 s on the laptop.

The small stones keep jostling instead of settling. One likely source:
small stones pressed into a boulder by the stones above (the speed rule
among small stones lets them overlap), which the position rule between
boulder and stone then pushes out each step. Not proven.

**Five tests tonight** (configs `configs/experiments/settle_*.toml`, the 2 m
copy of the stalled bed, one change each): at most 1,000 solver passes per
step (`settle_budget`); settling at 1 m/s² (`settle_g1`); both
(`settle_budget_g1`); the budget with every speed set to zero every 200 steps
until the first rest (`settle_budget_quench`); the budget with stones 10-20
cm long (`settle_budget_coarse`). Lower gravity is allowed because grains
without stickiness pack the same way at any gravity (only the time scale
changes), and the drops run at 0.00005 m/s² anyway. A method has to be at
least three times faster than today and pass the quality checks to make
bed B affordable. All five stop at 06:15 on 2 November, finished or not.
